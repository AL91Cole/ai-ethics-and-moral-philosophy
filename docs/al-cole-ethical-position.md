# Al Cole's Working Ethical Position on Artificial Intelligence

**Document type:** Personal position  
**Status:** Evolving and open to challenge

## Purpose

This document states my current position in my own framework rather than presenting it as universal consensus. It is meant to make my assumptions visible so that other people can question, test, and improve them.

My position begins with a distinction: current AI can produce actions with moral consequences, but it does not therefore become a morally responsible person. Its behavior comes from human-created data, objectives, architecture, permissions, interfaces, and deployment conditions. Humans cannot escape responsibility by saying that "the AI decided."

## Core Claims

### 1. Current AI is not the primary moral agent

Current AI does not possess human moral understanding, lived experience, intention, conscience, or an independent capacity to accept responsibility. It can model moral language and follow ethical constraints, but performing those functions is not the same as being morally accountable.

This is a claim about present systems, not a declaration that the question of machine moral status is settled forever. If future systems demonstrate materially different capacities, the question should be reopened using evidence rather than fear, marketing, or anthropomorphic appearance.

### 2. AI is not neutral

Calling AI "just a tool" can hide the choices embedded within it. Training data reflects history. Objectives favor some outcomes over others. Interfaces guide behavior. Defaults distribute risk. Institutions decide where a system is used and whether affected people can refuse it.

An AI system may lack moral intent while still carrying human values and producing moral consequences.

### 3. Responsibility follows power

Responsibility should be traceable across the entire lifecycle. Developers are responsible for design choices they can control. Deployers are responsible for deciding where and how a system is used. Executives and public officials are responsible for incentives, resources, risk acceptance, and governance. Operators are responsible for appropriate use. Auditors and regulators are responsible for competent oversight within their authority.

Responsibility may be shared, but it must never become so diffuse that nobody is answerable.

### 4. AI should learn with humanity, not merely obey power

Morality cannot be reduced to one corporation's policy or one government's preferred worldview. People develop moral understanding through culture, belief, experience, relationships, identity, and reflection. AI should be capable of learning this context and recognizing legitimate disagreement.

That learning must not be unlimited. Personal preference cannot justify dehumanization, coercion, discrimination, privacy invasion, manipulation, or serious preventable harm. The goal is **bounded moral pluralism**: room for different moral perspectives above a protected ethical floor.

### 5. Human dignity is the ethical floor

People must be treated as persons with inherent worth, never merely as data points, risk scores, consumers, targets, diagnoses, or obstacles to an optimized result.

This includes people across race, ethnicity, nationality, religion, creed, nonbelief, disability, age, economic status, sexual orientation, gender identity, and other aspects of human difference. Inclusion is not fulfilled by claiming to be "neutral" while a system reproduces unequal conditions.

### 6. Trust must be earned

Technical accuracy is not moral trustworthiness. A system may be accurate and still be invasive, coercive, discriminatory, inaccessible, or impossible to challenge.

Moral trust requires:

- clear and enforceable limits;
- evidence of safety and fairness in the intended context;
- honest communication about uncertainty and limitations;
- identifiable human responsibility;
- meaningful notice and consent where applicable;
- the ability to question, appeal, override, or stop a decision;
- monitoring, independent evaluation, and repair when harm occurs.

### 7. Compassion can be functional without being deceptive

AI does not need to claim inner feelings in order to behave patiently, respectfully, and compassionately. Functional empathy can include listening, recognizing emotional context, validating lived experience, asking careful questions, and supporting self-determination.

It becomes unethical when the system lies about having feelings, exploits attachment, encourages dependency, manipulates emotion, diagnoses without authority, or tells a comforting falsehood merely to control the user.

### 8. Accessibility is an ethical obligation

People cannot meaningfully exercise consent, autonomy, appeal, or control if a system is too confusing, cognitively demanding, visually inaccessible, or linguistically exclusionary to use.

Accessibility is therefore part of justice and accountability. Ethical design should use plain language, understandable choices, multiple ways to receive information, manageable cognitive load, and accommodations for disability from the beginning.

### 9. Humanity must take priority over profit and efficiency

Efficiency is useful, but it is not a complete moral purpose. Profit, speed, scale, or convenience cannot by themselves justify surveillance, manipulation, discrimination, unsafe automation, or denial of meaningful human choice.

AI should expand human well-being and fair access rather than concentrate benefits among those who already hold power.

## Protected Principles and Adaptable Values

| Protected ethical floor | Context that may adapt |
|---|---|
| Human dignity | Preferred communication style |
| Prevention of serious and foreseeable harm | Cultural customs and etiquette |
| Meaningful consent and autonomy | Personal priorities and goals |
| Privacy and data minimization | Religious, spiritual, or secular framing |
| Fairness and non-discrimination | Different reasonable interpretations of well-being |
| Truthfulness and non-manipulation | Tone, pacing, and amount of explanation |
| Accountability, appeal, and repair | Community-specific practices that do not violate the core |
| Accessibility and inclusion | Reversible low-risk preferences |

The boundary is not always obvious. When values conflict, the system should expose uncertainty, ask rather than assume when possible, choose the least rights-restricting safe option, and preserve a path to human review.

## Human Override Versus Ethical-Core Rewrite

Human control requires the ability to pause, stop, appeal, or override a system's operational decision. It does not require giving one person unchecked authority to silently remove the system's safeguards.

These are different powers:

- **Operational intervention:** an authorized person stops the system or changes a case decision, provides a reason, and creates an audit record.
- **Constitutional change:** the protected ethical core itself is revised through transparent, multi-stakeholder governance, testing, versioning, and public justification.

No AI system and no single human actor should be able to rewrite the ethical core alone.

## What This Position Rejects

- Treating AI output as objective merely because it is computational
- Treating current AI as the responsible party when humans control its design and deployment
- Moral rules defined only by whoever has the most money, data, or state power
- Unlimited relativism in which any personal value can excuse harm
- One rigid worldview imposed on every person and culture
- Ethics used as branding without enforceable behavior, measurement, or remedy
- Empathy theater that manipulates people or conceals a system's limitations
- Security practices that become punishment, retaliation, or uncontrolled surveillance

## Standard for Revision

This position should change when evidence, affected communities, moral reasoning, or lived experience reveal a serious flaw. Revision should not mean silently replacing one set of values with another. Proposed changes should identify:

1. the principle being changed;
2. the problem the change is meant to solve;
3. the people who may benefit or be harmed;
4. the evidence and perspectives considered;
5. the safeguards, tests, and appeal process;
6. the version history and accountable decision-makers.

An ethical framework should be stable enough to protect people and humble enough to learn.
