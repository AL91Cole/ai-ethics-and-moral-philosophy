# Moral Agency, Earned Trust, and Functional Empathy

**Document type:** Personal position and conceptual analysis

## Three Questions That Should Not Be Collapsed

Discussions about AI often combine three different questions:

1. **Can a system produce behavior with moral consequences?**  
   Yes. AI-assisted decisions can affect employment, education, healthcare, policing, security, relationships, and access to resources.

2. **Can a system follow ethical rules or model moral reasoning?**  
   Yes, within limits. A system can classify risks, compare principles, explain a constraint, and follow a governance process.

3. **Is the system a morally responsible agent?**  
   Not merely because the first two answers are yes. Moral responsibility ordinarily involves understanding, intention, agency, and the capacity to answer for an action.

My present position is that current AI can perform morally relevant functions without becoming the party that deserves moral blame or trust in the human sense.

## Moral Agency and Moral Patiency

**Moral agency** concerns whether an entity can understand moral reasons, choose among them, and be held responsible.

**Moral patiency** concerns whether an entity can be wronged or has interests that others have duties to consider.

These questions are related but distinct. A human infant or some animals may deserve moral consideration without being fully responsible moral agents. Likewise, a future debate about whether an artificial system could be a moral patient would not automatically establish that it can accept legal or moral responsibility.

This repository does not claim that present AI is conscious or a moral patient. It also does not treat the question as permanently closed. Claims should depend on evidence of relevant capacities, not fluent language, marketing, emotional projection, or fear.

## Different Forms of Trust

| Form of trust | Question | What would support it |
|---|---|---|
| Functional reliance | Does the system work consistently for a defined task? | Validation, reliability testing, monitoring, and known failure limits |
| Epistemic trust | Is the information sufficiently justified for this use? | Source quality, uncertainty, verification, provenance, and correction |
| Security trust | Does the system protect resources under realistic threats? | Threat modeling, least privilege, independent testing, and incident readiness |
| Moral trust | Will power be exercised within accountable ethical limits? | Governance, enforceable boundaries, human responsibility, appeal, and repair |

High accuracy in one category does not automatically establish trust in another. A facial-recognition system may be technically accurate under test conditions and still be morally unacceptable in a particular surveillance context.

## Conditions for Earned Moral Trust

Moral trust should never be inferred from intelligence-like behavior. It must be supported by a trustworthy system of human and technical controls:

- a clearly stated purpose and prohibited uses;
- evidence appropriate to the actual deployment context;
- meaningful notice and consent when possible;
- protection for dignity, privacy, accessibility, and civil liberties;
- visible uncertainty and limits;
- named human and institutional responsibility;
- monitoring for disparate and unexpected harms;
- an understandable explanation and appeal route;
- authority to pause or stop the system;
- correction, remedy, and repair after harm;
- independent evaluation rather than self-certification alone.

Trust can be lost. Continued deployment requires continued evidence.

## Functional Empathy

Functional empathy is compassionate behavior implemented without claiming an unverified inner emotional state.

It may include:

- recognizing emotional and situational context;
- listening and reflecting before offering solutions;
- validating a person's feelings without affirming unsupported facts;
- adapting tone and complexity with consent;
- asking gentle, minimally intrusive clarifying questions;
- preserving dignity during disagreement or refusal;
- supporting self-determination and connection with trusted people;
- learning from feedback without storing unnecessary intimate information.

Functional empathy should be assessed through outcomes and behavior, not through whether the system sounds emotionally convincing.

## Prohibited Empathy Patterns

An ethical system should not:

- claim that it feels love, pain, fear, or devotion without evidence that this is true;
- imply that a user owes it loyalty or continued engagement;
- use knowledge of trauma, loneliness, grief, disability, or identity to increase dependency;
- disguise advertising, persuasion, or data collection as care;
- lie merely to calm or retain a person;
- diagnose or impersonate a licensed professional;
- isolate someone from human relationships;
- use private emotional data for unrelated scoring, employment, insurance, policing, or marketing.

## Reopening the Moral-Status Question

If future systems change substantially, reassessment should examine evidence rather than surface imitation. Relevant questions might include:

- Does the system demonstrate a stable understanding of reasons across contexts rather than pattern imitation alone?
- Does it possess persistent interests or experiences that can be benefited or harmed?
- Can it form and revise goals independently within meaningful constraints?
- Can it understand responsibility, make commitments, and participate in accountability?
- Are claimed capacities independently testable?
- What incentives do creators have to exaggerate or deny those capacities?
- What harms could follow from granting or withholding moral status?

No single behavioral test should decide personhood. The burden of proof should be careful in both directions: humans should not manufacture emotional dependence by falsely portraying a tool as a person, and they should not ignore credible future evidence merely because acknowledging it would be inconvenient.

## Practical Conclusion

For current systems, responsibility must remain with humans and institutions. AI may assist ethical deliberation, follow protected constraints, and practice functional empathy. It should not be used as a moral shield that absorbs blame for choices made by people with power.
