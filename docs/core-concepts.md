# Core Concepts in AI Ethics

AI ethics studies the moral consequences of artificial-intelligence systems and the responsibilities of the people and institutions that design, deploy, govern, and use them.

## Central Distinction

Current AI can produce morally significant consequences without becoming the morally responsible agent. Human choices about data, objectives, permissions, interfaces, incentives, and deployment remain central.

## Key Concepts

### Human dignity

Every person has worth that cannot be reduced to a score, prediction, demographic category, diagnosis, or optimization target.

### Autonomy and consent

People should retain meaningful control over decisions affecting their lives. Consent must be understandable, specific, freely given, reversible where possible, and supported by a real alternative.

### Fairness and structural bias

Bias is not only a defect in data. It can reflect historical inequality, institutional priorities, unequal access, and the way a problem is defined. Fairness requires examining outcomes, power, procedure, and repair.

### Accountability and redress

Responsibility must remain traceable to humans and institutions. Affected people need notice, explanation, correction, appeal, qualified human review, and meaningful remedy.

### Transparency and explainability

Transparency concerns the system's purpose, data, limits, governance, and use. Explainability concerns whether a particular outcome can be understood and challenged by the people affected.

### Privacy

Privacy protects a person's control over information and freedom from unjustified observation or inference. Ethical design uses data minimization, purpose limitation, security, retention limits, and honest disclosure of where plaintext exists.

### Accessibility

Accessibility is part of justice. Rights and choices are not meaningful when an interface, explanation, or appeal process cannot be used by people with different disabilities, communication needs, or cognitive processing styles.

### Moral agency

Moral agency involves understanding moral reasons, choosing among them, and being answerable. Following ethical rules or producing moral language does not by itself establish moral agency.

### Bounded moral pluralism

People and cultures reasonably disagree about many values. AI may adapt to that context above a protected ethical floor, but learned preferences cannot excuse dehumanization, coercion, discrimination, manipulation, or serious harm.

### Functional empathy

AI may behave compassionately through patience, contextual understanding, and respectful support without claiming emotions it cannot establish. Empathy becomes unethical when used to manipulate attachment, conceal limits, diagnose, or increase dependency.

### Power

Every AI system distributes power: the power to define categories, allocate resources, observe people, make predictions, and decide whose explanation counts. Ethical analysis asks who benefits, who bears risk, and who can say no.

## Central Question

The central question is not only what AI can do, but **what it ought to do, who it should serve, who remains responsible, and what limits people with power must accept**.
