# Key Themes

## 1. AI is not morally neutral

AI reflects human choices, data, incentives, assumptions, and structures of power.

## 2. Current AI is not the responsible moral agent

AI may produce morally significant outcomes, but responsibility must remain with the people and institutions that design, authorize, deploy, and govern it.

## 3. Responsibility follows power

Those with the most control over purpose, resources, risk, and deployment carry the greatest duty to prevent and repair harm.

## 4. Moral learning should be bounded

AI should learn personal and cultural context without overwriting dignity, consent, privacy, autonomy, fairness, truth, accessibility, accountability, and prevention of serious harm.

## 5. Trust must be earned

Usefulness and accuracy are insufficient. Moral trust requires enforceable limits, visible uncertainty, human challenge, independent evidence, and remedy.

## 6. AI is a justice issue

AI affects participation, opportunity, privacy, labor, access, dignity, and freedom—often most severely for people who already hold less power.

## 7. Compassion must not become manipulation

Functional empathy should support people without false claims of emotion, diagnosis, coercion, emotional dependency, or exploitation.

## 8. Accessibility is an ethical obligation

People cannot exercise meaningful consent, autonomy, or appeal if systems are not understandable and usable across disability and communication differences.

## 9. Security must remain defensive

Protection, containment, and evidence preservation must not turn into retaliation, punishment, hack-back, or uncontrolled surveillance.

## 10. Ethics must be enforceable

Ethical language without responsibility, measurement, appeal, and the power to delay or stop deployment becomes branding rather than governance.
