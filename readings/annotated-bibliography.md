# AI Ethics and Moral Philosophy: Annotated Bibliography

**Document type:** Source analysis  
**Scope:** Sources used in the original final paper, followed by later standards used for the applied AI Guardian crosswalk

## Purpose

This bibliography separates what a source argues from my own interpretation and from the design proposals developed elsewhere in this repository. The first three books directly informed the original paper, *Human Power, Accountability, and the Ethics of AI*. The standards and legal materials were added later to translate the philosophical position into governance and security questions.

## Sources Used in the Original Paper

### Eubanks, Virginia

**Citation**  
Eubanks, V. (2018). *Automating inequality: How high-tech tools profile, police, and punish the poor*. St. Martin's Press.

**Central argument**  
Eubanks examines automated decision systems used in public services and shows how data-driven administration can intensify surveillance, exclusion, and punishment for poor and working-class people. The technology does not remove policy choices; it can hide them behind technical authority.

**Ethical significance**  
The book moves AI ethics beyond abstract bias and into institutions that determine access to essential resources. It demonstrates that error and efficiency cannot be evaluated separately from power, poverty, and the lived consequences of being classified by a system that is difficult to challenge.

**Connection to my position**  
This source supports my claim that the deeper ethical problem is human and institutional power. Automation can move authority away from affected people while making decisions appear objective. It strengthens my emphasis on fair access, meaningful explanation, appeal, human alternatives, and corrective justice.

**Questions and limitations for this project**  
The source strongly diagnoses institutional harm but does not by itself provide a complete technical architecture for bounded moral learning, privacy-first companions, or defensive deception. Those applications require additional sources and testing.

### Mitchell, Melanie

**Citation**  
Mitchell, M. (2019). *Artificial intelligence: A guide for thinking humans*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.

**Central argument**  
Mitchell explains the capabilities and limitations of modern AI and cautions against confusing pattern recognition or fluent output with broad human understanding. AI performance can be powerful within a task while remaining brittle outside the conditions represented in training and evaluation.

**Ethical significance**  
Overestimating AI can encourage people to assign systems authority they have not earned. Anthropomorphic language can also hide the human choices that define objectives, data, and deployment.

**Connection to my position**  
This source supports my distinction between morally relevant behavior and moral agency. A system may follow constraints or model ethical language without possessing human moral understanding, intention, or accountability. It also supports caution in claims about AI empathy, consciousness, and independent moral judgment.

**Questions and limitations for this project**  
My framework remains open to AI learning alongside people. Mitchell's caution creates an important test: adaptive moral language must not be mistaken for moral comprehension. The design must make uncertainty and human responsibility visible.

### O'Neil, Cathy

**Citation**  
O'Neil, C. (2016). *Weapons of math destruction: How big data increases inequality and threatens democracy*. Crown.

**Central argument**  
O'Neil describes large-scale mathematical models that are opaque, consequential, and difficult for affected people to contest. These systems can create feedback loops in employment, education, credit, policing, and other domains while presenting value-laden choices as objective measurement.

**Ethical significance**  
Scale can transform a flawed assumption into widespread structural harm. Opacity also weakens accountability when institutions blame a model rather than the people who chose its variables, objectives, and use.

**Connection to my position**  
The source supports my claims that AI is not neutral, responsibility must not disappear into the system, and technical accuracy cannot establish fairness. It also supports requirements for transparency, appeal, correction, outcome monitoring, and named human ownership.

**Questions and limitations for this project**  
Opening a model is not always sufficient or safe, and a technical explanation does not necessarily provide justice. The repository therefore distinguishes transparency from meaningful explanation and pairs both with redress.

## Later Applied-Governance References

The following sources were **not citations in the original submitted paper**. They support the later standards crosswalk and applied AI Guardian analysis.

### National Institute of Standards and Technology: AI RMF

**Citation**  
National Institute of Standards and Technology. (2023). *Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)* (NIST AI 100-1). https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1

**Relevance**  
The AI RMF organizes AI risk work through `GOVERN`, `MAP`, `MEASURE`, and `MANAGE` and describes characteristics of trustworthy AI. It provides a practical structure for connecting protected values with lifecycle governance, contextual impact analysis, evaluation, risk response, and documentation.

**Caution**  
The framework is voluntary and outcome-oriented. Mapping a personal principle to an AI RMF function does not prove that the principle is implemented effectively.

### National Institute of Standards and Technology: Generative AI Profile

**Citation**  
National Institute of Standards and Technology. (2024). *Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile* (NIST AI 600-1). https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1

**Relevance**  
This profile extends AI RMF analysis to risks associated with generative AI. It is relevant to truth, confabulation, harmful content, privacy, security, human-AI interaction, and the evaluation of systems that produce persuasive language.

### Rose, Borchert, Mitchell, and Connelly: Zero Trust

**Citation**  
Rose, S., Borchert, O., Mitchell, S., & Connelly, S. (2020). *Zero trust architecture* (NIST SP 800-207). National Institute of Standards and Technology. https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.800-207

**Relevance**  
NIST SP 800-207 defines zero-trust concepts that remove implicit trust based on network location or ownership and focus protection on resources. It informs AI Guardian's explicit authentication, authorization, least privilege, resource isolation, and continuous evaluation.

**Caution**  
Zero trust is a security architecture, not a complete moral theory. It does not by itself resolve surveillance proportionality, accessibility, civil liberties, moral learning, or redress.

### Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency: Zero Trust Maturity Model

**Citation**  
Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. (2023). *Zero Trust Maturity Model* (Version 2.0). https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/zero-trust-maturity-model

**Relevance**  
CISA's maturity model applies zero trust across identity, devices, networks, applications and workloads, and data, supported by visibility and analytics, automation and orchestration, and governance. It helps organize AI Guardian capabilities while leaving room for the repository's added ethical restrictions.

### National Institute of Standards and Technology: Cybersecurity Framework

**Citation**  
National Institute of Standards and Technology. (2024). *The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0* (NIST CSWP 29). https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.CSWP.29

**Relevance**  
CSF 2.0's `GOVERN`, `IDENTIFY`, `PROTECT`, `DETECT`, `RESPOND`, and `RECOVER` functions connect institutional responsibility with the full cybersecurity lifecycle. This is useful for ensuring that Guardian ethics are not separated from operational risk and recovery.

### European Union: Artificial Intelligence Act

**Citation**  
European Parliament and Council of the European Union. (2024). Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence. *Official Journal of the European Union*. https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj

**Relevance**  
The Act provides a risk-based legal context involving prohibited practices, high-risk systems, transparency, human oversight, governance, robustness, and fundamental rights.

**Caution**  
This repository does not provide a legal applicability or compliance determination. Duties depend on current law, role, jurisdiction, use case, and implementation facts.

## Themes Across the Sources

The sources converge on several issues:

- AI capability is often mistaken for understanding or objectivity.
- Models carry human assumptions and institutional purposes.
- Large-scale automation can reproduce and hide inequality.
- People with the least power may experience the greatest consequences.
- Accountability requires governance, not only technical improvement.
- Security and risk frameworks can structure action but do not settle moral disagreement.

## Research Gaps

The source base should expand before this repository is treated as a mature philosophical framework. Future reading should include:

- disability justice and accessible technology;
- feminist and care ethics;
- racial justice and critical algorithm studies;
- decolonial ethics and Indigenous data sovereignty;
- global and non-Western moral traditions;
- labor, automation, and economic justice;
- privacy, surveillance, and civil liberties;
- trauma-informed technology and emotional dependency;
- philosophy of mind and machine moral status;
- environmental and supply-chain justice;
- empirical evaluation of AI governance and redress.

Adding sources should deepen or challenge the argument, not merely decorate conclusions already reached.
